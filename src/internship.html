Can a machine hear empathy?

A transcript records the words but not how they were spoken. At NBME I led a research project on whether the sound of a medical student's voice carries what human listeners judge as empathy, and whether audio AI models judge it the way people do.

Where this matters

NBME co-sponsors the exams used to license medical doctors in the United States. One of its newer tools, the Communication Learning Assessment, helps medical students practice patient-centered communication: the learner watches a recorded vignette of a simulated patient and records a spoken reply.

Scoring speech at scale usually starts by converting it to text, because text is what language models read best. For communication skills that first step loses information. Part of empathy may be in the delivery, and transcription removes the delivery.

So the project asked two things. Do measurable properties of the voice relate to how empathic human listeners judge a response to be? And when an audio AI model is asked to make the same judgment, does it agree with people?

How we went after it

The study makes one comparison: each spoken response gets a human judgment, and each automated route is measured by how closely it matches that judgment.

People first

Empathy is a judgment, and two listeners can hear the same reply differently. I directed the eight-person rating team and was one of its raters. Part of the data was rated twice, which shows how much humans agree among themselves. That agreement is the reference for the models.

Measure the voice

I built Python pipelines that turn each recording into acoustic and paralinguistic features, then trained classification and regression models to predict the human ratings from those numbers alone. This route is transparent: you can see which properties of the voice a model leans on.

Ask the models

I benchmarked open-source audio language models (Qwen) against a closed-source one (GPT-4o Audio), with prompts that gave the model either no examples (zero-shot) or a few human-rated examples (few-shot), using the audio and the transcript. I also fine-tuned a text-based language model on the conversation transcripts.

What "agreeing with humans" means

The whole study rests on measuring agreement, and two raters can agree in more than one sense. Rater A's scores for eight made-up clips are fixed below. Set rater B's scores and watch two standard measures respond.

Weighted kappa asks whether the two give the same scores, beyond what chance would produce, and forgives a near miss more than a far one. 1 is perfect, 0 is chance.
Spearman correlation asks only whether they put the clips in the same order.

How the work ran

I did the work in Python on AWS, with peer code review and Agile planning in Jira, compared the explanations the models wrote as well as their scores, and presented the work to the company and to stakeholders. The manuscript, with me as first author, is submitted to ICASSP 2027.

This is close to my PhD work. In the lab I study what the brain and body show about listening that a word score misses. Here the question was what a voice might carry that a transcript misses.
