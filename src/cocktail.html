Decoding where you look and listen at a party

In a crowded room you follow one voice and ignore the rest. I asked whether a cap that shines near-infrared light through the scalp can tell, from a single trial a few seconds long, which side a listener is attending to, and which part of the brain carries that information.

Step 1

The problem: one voice among many

Following one talker while others talk over them is called the cocktail party problem. Most people do it without noticing. For people with hearing loss it is one of the hardest things about daily life, and hearing aids do not solve it: they cannot know which voice you want, so competing voices get amplified along with it.

A device could do better if it knew which direction its wearer was attending to. Then it could favor sound from that direction. The listener's brain already holds that information. Can it be read from outside the head, quickly, from one short stretch of listening, with something a person could wear?

I used fNIRS (functional near-infrared spectroscopy), a brain imaging method that is portable, silent, and needs nothing more invasive than a cap. Most earlier attempts used EEG, which measures electrical activity and is fast but hard to pin to a location in the brain. fNIRS is slower and shows more clearly where a signal comes from, which matters if the goal is to shrink the device to only the part that is needed.

In plain wordsCan a cap tell which way your attention is pointing, and can most of the cap then be thrown away?

Step 2

The experiment

A listener sits with their chin on a rest, facing three screens, wearing insert earphones, eye-tracking glasses, and the fNIRS cap. Every trial runs the same way. A two-second burst of noise comes from the left or the right: that is the cue. Then two people talk at once for three seconds, one from 30° to the left and one from 30° to the right. The listener follows the one on the cued side and afterwards picks what that talker said from three choices. Answering and resting take 10 to 12 seconds, and then the next trial begins.

The three choices follow the real experiment: one is what your talker said, one is what the other talker said, and one is something neither said. Only trials answered correctly, with the eyes where they were supposed to be, were analyzed, so every trial that reached the decoder is one where the listener was following the cued side.

There were two versions of the task. In look and listen, a crosshair marked the cued screen, each talker appeared as a video on their screen, and listeners turned their eyes to the cued one, as you would in a real conversation. In listen only, there was no video, the same audio played, and listeners kept their eyes on a crosshair on the center screen. Each listener did 60 trials of each. The main result depends on the difference between these two versions.

Step 3

The sounds: placing a voice in space with two earphones

Earphones sit inside the ear canals, so how can a voice seem to come from 30° to the left? Because your brain locates sound from the differences between what your two ears receive. A voice on your left reaches the left ear a fraction of a millisecond earlier, and reaches the right ear quieter and duller, because your head is in the way.

Those differences can be measured once, with microphones in the ears of an acoustic manikin, and stored as a pair of filters for each direction. Passing any sound through the pair for −30° makes it arrive at your ears the way a real source at −30° would. Drag the source around and listen. These are the filters used to build the experiment's stimuli.

Step 4

Measuring the brain with light

Hold a flashlight behind your fingers and they glow red. Skin and bone let red and near-infrared light through, and what stops that light is mostly blood. fNIRS is built on those two facts. A small light source sits on the scalp. A detector sits a few centimeters away. Light entering the head does not travel straight: it scatters off tissue like headlights in fog, and a tiny fraction wanders down, through the skull, and back up to the detector.

The light that makes that trip has sampled a banana-shaped region between source and detector. Slide the detector away from the source and watch what happens to the banana, and to how much light reaches the detector.

Your turnA simplified 2D simulation. It shows the trend, not exact tissue values.

Close pairs see only the scalp. Far pairs reach the brain but collect very little light. The cap uses both on purpose: the close ones measure blood flow in the scalp so it can be subtracted from the far ones.

Two colors for two kinds of blood. Active brain cells need oxygen, and within a few seconds the local blood supply over-delivers: oxygen-rich hemoglobin (HbO) goes up and oxygen-poor hemoglobin (HbR) goes down. The two absorb light differently. At 760 nm, HbR absorbs roughly two and a half times as strongly as HbO. At 850 nm it is HbO that absorbs more. Measuring at both colors gives two readings for two unknowns, so both can be solved for.

Hold the button to make this patch of brain work, and watch the blood respond. It is slow, and both the experiment and the decoder had to be designed around that delay.

Step 5

From raw light to a brain signal

What comes off a detector is not a brain signal yet. It is brightness over time, and the brain's contribution is the smallest thing in it. Your pulse is in there. So are slow drifts, jolts when the head moves, and blood flow in the scalp, which changes with breathing and blood pressure and has nothing to do with listening. Each step of the processing removes one of these. Switch them on one at a time.

Your turnA simulated channel, built to show what each step removes

The real pipeline, in order: drop channels that are too dim or saturated; convert brightness to absorption; repair motion artifacts (a method called TDDR); band-pass filter between 0.01 and 0.5 Hz; convert the two colors into HbO and HbR; then fit a model of each trial's response while regressing out the average of the short channels, which carry the scalp signal. Each trial is measured relative to the two seconds before its cue.

Step 6

Teaching a machine to read one trial

Averaging 60 trials gives a clean picture, but a hearing device cannot wait for 60 trials. It has to decide from one. That is a machine learning problem: show a model many trials labeled "attending left" or "attending right," let it find what separates them, then test it on trials it has never seen.

The decoder in the paper does the same job with more to work with. For each listener it:

Picks its channels

From the usable channels (567 at most), it keeps the 20 whose signals differ most between left and right trials, measured by how far apart the two groups sit compared with how much each one spreads.

Summarizes each second

Those 20 channels are compressed into up to ten combined signals. For every one-second window, each signal is reduced to two numbers: how high it got and how steeply it was changing.

Lets a forest vote

A random forest of 300 small decision trees each looks at the numbers and votes left or right. The majority wins. Many weak, varied votes add up to a stable answer.

Testing it honestly. A model that is graded on trials it trained on will always look good. So the trials are split into five parts: the model learns from four and is graded on the fifth, and this rotates until every trial has been a test trial. The whole thing is repeated ten times with different splits. Everything the model learns, including which 20 channels to keep, is learned from the training part alone.

What counts as better than guessing? With two choices, guessing gets 50% in the long run. But a listener has at most 60 trials (38 to 60 once unusable ones are removed), and on that few a pure guesser can get lucky. Try it with 54.

Step 7

What it found

When listeners looked and listened, one trial was enough for most of them. Across 26 listeners the decoder's mean peak accuracy was 76.5%, and 21 of the 26 were above chance. Each dot below is one listener.

For one listener, here is how accuracy unfolds within a trial. It climbs within the first couple of seconds after the cue, holds while the talkers are on, and fades as the trial ends.

Where in the brain was it reading? Because the cap covers the whole head, the decoder's choices can be traced back to brain regions. Two stood out, one on each side: the left and right inferior parietal lobule (IPL), toward the back of the head above and behind the ear. They were the only regions the decoder used in every listener it worked for.

Share of the decoder's information drawn from each region, averaged over the above-chance listeners in whom that region contributed. The two IPL regions (measured over the angular gyrus) are highlighted.

Was the decoder reading attention or eye position? In the look-and-listen task, listeners did two things at once. They attended to one side and they looked at it. The first chart above already suggests which: with the same sounds but the eyes held at center, accuracy fell to 55.9%, inside the chance band.

To pin it down, eight listeners came back for a control experiment. At the cue they turned their eyes to one side, then held them there for 15 to 17 seconds before the talkers came on. If the IPL signal came from the eye movement, it should rise briefly and fade. Instead it persisted.

Each side of the brain responds when the eyes point to the opposite side, and keeps responding for as long as they stay there. So the IPL signal tracks where the eyes are pointing, not the act of moving them. And the listen-only task shows that listening alone was not enough. That is less of a limitation than it sounds. In real conversation people look at who they are listening to, so gaze direction is a strong, steady sign of where attention is, and it can be read from one compact region.

In plain wordsThe cap can tell which side you are looking and listening toward from a single trial. The signal follows your gaze, and it comes from one small area on each side of the head.

Step 8

The path to a smaller cap

A cap with 200 optodes is a research instrument that nobody would wear in daily life. But if the signal that matters most sits over the IPL, most of the cap may not be needed. So the decoder was run again using only the channels over the left and right IPL: 58 of the 567, about a tenth of the cap.

Your turn

On average accuracy barely moved: 76.5% with everything, 74.4% with the IPL alone, a difference that is not statistically significant (paired t-test, p = 0.29), though three fewer listeners cleared chance. A tenth of the cap was enough for comparable decoding.

That points to a different kind of device: a compact patch of optodes over the IPL on each side instead of a full cap, reading which side the wearer is oriented toward and passing that to a hearing device that favors sound from there.

What this does not show yet

  • Listeners sat still with their chin on a rest. Real use means head movement, which fNIRS has to be made robust to.
  • Attention and gaze moved together in the task that decoded well. With the eyes fixed, decoding was near chance, so this is not yet a read-out of attention when someone listens to one side while looking elsewhere.
  • The choice was only left or right, announced by a cue, in separate trials with rest in between. Continuous listening needs a different way to set the baseline.
  • Each listener's decoder was trained on that listener's own data.
  • The IPL-only result was computed from the full cap's data. A compact device has not been built or tested.
