Listening effort

Two people can both repeat every word of a sentence heard in noise, one with ease and the other only by straining. A word score rates them the same. This project asks how to measure the difference.

The problem with counting words

Hearing aids, earbuds, and speech enhancement software are tuned and scored on intelligibility: how many words a listener gets right. That number leaves out something everyone knows from a loud restaurant. You can catch every word across the table and still go home worn out.

What is missing is called listening effort: the mental resources a person spends to understand what they hear [1]. Two listeners, or two devices, can reach the same word score at very different cost. A device that is scored only on words gets no credit for making listening easier and no penalty for making it harder.

In plain wordsGetting the words right shows that someone understood, but not how hard it was.

The sentences come from a matrix test: always a name, a verb, a number, an adjective, and an object, with ten choices for each. Every sentence is equally predictable, so nobody can fill in a missed word from context, and scoring is a count out of five. The rating uses a 13-step listening effort scale from the hearing research literature [2], with a fourteenth option for when all you heard was noise.

How we are thinking about it

We take three measures from the same few seconds of listening, and each captures something the others miss.

What you got

Words correct out of five. Objective and simple, and the number the field already uses. It measures the outcome and says nothing about the cost.

What it felt like

The effort rating. It is the listener's own account of the cost, but it interrupts the listening to ask, and people use scales differently.

What your body did

The pupil widens when the brain works harder [3], and degraded speech draws on brain areas beyond the ones that process sound [4]. Neither needs the listener to say anything.

That is why the goal is a physiological read-out of listening effort: a signal that could be used to evaluate, and eventually steer, audio processing without stopping the listener to ask. The behavioral measures are there to anchor it. If a body signal is going to stand in for effort, it has to be checked against what listeners got and what they said it cost.

How the experiment is built

The scene. The three talkers are placed in virtual space over insert earphones, using filters measured on an acoustic manikin's ears so that each voice arrives the way it would from that direction. Difficulty is set by one knob, the level of the target against the maskers: −5, 0, or +5 dB.

Three kinds of trial. Reporting words and clicking a rating move the eyes and the hands, and that activity would be mixed into any body signal recorded at the same time. So the trials come in three kinds, interleaved and unannounced. Pick one to see what it is for.

The recording. Listeners wear a whole-head fNIRS cap with 56 light sources and 144 detectors, which tracks blood oxygen changes across the surface of the brain using near-infrared light (the cocktail party page explains how that works). Eye-tracking glasses measure pupil size at the same time. The screen stays a constant dim gray throughout, because a pupil also reacts to brightness, and that reaction would swamp the one we are after. A hardware trigger marks every trial in the fNIRS recording, and software markers put the eye tracker on the same clock, so brain, pupil, and sound line up.

Where it fits. The first project of my thesis decoded which side a listener was attending to. This one asks how hard they are working to listen, in the same kind of multi-talker scene and with the same whole-head system.
